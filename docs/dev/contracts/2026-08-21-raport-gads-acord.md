# The Google Ads report says the same thing to one product as it does to ten

    objective: A prospect reading our Google Ads report never meets a sentence that argues with its own number, or a headline count that disagrees with the cards under it — and no future round can put either back without something refusing.

    results:
    1. A prospect never reads a sentence that fights the number inside it. Today the report he gets after connecting his account has ten such sentences, and the public report anyone can generate has up to twenty-two more.
    2. The headline count of what costs him money agrees with the cards beneath it. Today the header says five while the fifth card writes, in its own words, that it cost nothing.
    3. A sentence that disagrees with its number cannot come back in silence. Today nothing anywhere requires this text to agree: all ten wrong sentences passed the type-checker, the linter and 254 green tests without one of them noticing.
    4. The report stops being right or wrong depending on whose account it is. Today three sentences are correct for a shop with forty products and wrong for a shop with twelve — the same line, the same code, a different client.
    5. Everything above is on the live site and was read there. Today none of it is: the live site has been serving the same code since before any of this was found.

## How to read this

The numbered rows are for whoever decides whether this work is worth doing. They carry no file names, no
line numbers and no code. Everything a person needs in order to DO the work — where each thing lives and
which command proves it — is in the appendix at the bottom.

Sentences quoted from the report are quoted exactly as a client sees them, in the language the client
reads them in. They are evidence, not prose.

**Money status of this contract: no row moves money.** The tool this contract governs only ever READS a
Google Ads account — that is the first invariant of the skill that owns it, and nothing here touches a
budget, a bid or a campaign. By the ruling of 19 August, that makes every row deploy-first and
review-on-production. One asymmetry is worth knowing and is not a money question: the public report can be
generated by a stranger with no account, so a defect there reaches people the moment it ships, while the
connected report is only reached by someone who has just handed us access.

**Where the vocabulary comes from.** Two closed lists govern this contract and neither exists yet, so
defining them is the first delivery of the rows that need them. `moneySign` says whether a finding's money
is a loss, a gain, or a figure we deliberately did not put a number on. `numberForm` says which of three
shapes a sentence takes for a given count. The second one is the contract writer's proposal, not a
reviewer's finding and not the operator's instruction — it is labelled so it can be overruled cheaply.

## 1. Nothing in the report may disagree with its own number, and something must refuse it

**What this is.** Our Google Ads report is built out of "findings" — short cards, each with a title and a
paragraph, saying what is wrong with the account and what it costs. Almost every one of them has a count
written into the sentence: how many products, how many campaigns, how many searches. The sentence is
written once and has to read correctly whether that count turns out to be one, twelve or four hundred.

**Problem.** Nothing requires those sentences to agree with the numbers inside them. Whether a given
sentence is right is decided by whoever typed it, and there is no way to find out except by reading the
report with that exact account's data in front of you.

**Today.** Rendered at one and at two, thirty-three sentences in the connected report were checked: twenty-three
read correctly at both, ten do not. The ten were invisible to the type-checker, to the linter and to 254
passing tests. Repairing them one at a time leaves the twenty-fourth unguarded — which is not a
hypothetical: a third file, which neither review opened, was found to hold up to twenty-two more of the
same shape.

**Fix.** Every finding the report can produce gets rendered at a count of one and at a count of two before
anything ships, and a sentence whose form does not match its count is refused by name. The three forms a
count can take in this language become a named list with three values instead of a decision each author
makes again: one thing, a few things, and — from twenty upward — the form that takes the extra particle.

**Done when.** Run against the code that is on the live site: the check names every one of the ten known
wrong sentences and passes on all twenty-three known good ones. Then one of the ten is put back exactly as
it was, the check runs again and FAILS naming that line, and is then restored. A check that only ever
passes is not a check.

**Proving this creates nothing** — it runs against the code, touches no account and writes nothing.

**This row feeds rows 3 and 4.** It is first for that reason, not because it is the most visible: on its
own a client sees nothing change.

## 2. The headline count of what costs money counts what it labels

**What this is.** The report opens with a summary bar. One of its figures is the number of findings that
cost the client money, and directly under it sit the finding cards themselves, numbered.

**Problem.** The number in the header and the cards under it are worked out separately, so they can
disagree — and they do. Underneath, whether a sum is money lost or money that could be gained is decided
in three different places by three different rules, none of which says so out loud.

**Today.** On the demonstration account the header says five. The fifth card writes in its own text that it
cost nothing — we deliberately do not invent a figure for it. An attempt to repair this on 21 August made
it worse and was withdrawn: filtering to findings with a sum pulled in the projected GAIN of 81.000 RON,
which the report itself prints in green with a plus sign, and left the header saying four above five
numbered cards. Two independent reviews measured that, and the withdrawal is on the record with its reason.
The honest number on that account is three.

**Fix.** Whether a finding's money is a loss, a gain, or a figure we chose not to put a number on stops
being guessed and becomes something each finding declares. The header then counts the losses, and the
colour and the plus sign read the same declaration instead of each working it out again.

**Done when.** The test that already exists for this — written by one of the reviewers and proved on three
arms before this contract was written — passes on the live code. It compares the number printed immediately
before the header's label against the number of cards showing a sum with no plus in front of it, and
requires them to be equal. With the original defect it reads five against three and fails; with the
withdrawn attempt it reads four against three and fails; with the repair it reads three against three and
passes. All three arms are run, and the failing two are shown failing.

Then the report is opened on the live site and the header figure is quoted beside the count of cards under
it, and they agree.

**Proving this creates nothing on our side.** How a report is obtained on the live site is question 1
below, and until that is answered the second half of this row cannot be closed.

## 3. Every sentence in the connected flow agrees with its own number

**What this is.** After a prospect connects his Google Ads account he is asked one question about his
margin, and then shown the report. Twelve sentences across those two screens put a count into a sentence
that does not adjust to it.

**Problem.** At certain counts the report tells a client something that is plainly wrong in his own
language, on the screen where we are asking him to trust our numbers.

**Today.** Twelve places, each read from an actual render:

- the first sentence he sees after connecting, before any report: `Am citit 1 produse din contul tau`
- `1 cautari au consumat buget fara nicio vanzare`
- `Sunt 1 campanii in cont, dar niciuna nu a livrat.`
- `1 campanii de tip care arde buget fara sa vanda`
- `1 campanii oprite se descurcau mai bine decat media contului`
- `2 reclame de tip vechi sunt inca pornita in cont` — this one breaks at two and above, not at one
- two cards whose title is written for one thing while the paragraph under it is written for several — four
  disagreements inside one paragraph in the first, five in the second
- the shared table footer, when a list is cut short: `si inca 1 produse`
- three sentences that are correct or wrong depending on the account's data, because in this language the
  extra particle appears only from twenty upward: `Bugetul tau e intins pe de 7 de ori mai multe produse`,
  `reclamele s-au afisat pentru 12 de produse`, `bugetul tau poate duce asa in jur de 6 de produse`. Written
  for a shop with forty products, wrong for a shop with twelve.

**Fix.** All twelve are rewritten so the whole sentence agrees, not only the noun — the verb, the
participle and the demonstrative change with the count as well. A helper that fixes only the noun was
rejected by both reviews and by the delivery that already tried it: it leaves behind sentences like
`1 produs nu au fost vazute`, which look repaired and are not.

**Done when.** The check from row 1 passes on all twelve. Then, on the live site, the first sentence after
connecting is read at a count of one and quoted with what it says.

**Proving this creates nothing on the account** — the tool only reads. Obtaining a live report is question 1.

## 4. The public report, which anyone can generate, has the same defect and nobody had opened it — OPEN

**What this is.** Besides the connected report, we publish a free report anyone can run against their own
website without giving us any access. It is our lead magnet: it is the first thing a stranger reads from
us, and it is generated by a different engine from the connected one.

**Problem.** The same class of sentence lives there and nobody has looked, because both reviews were
pointed at the connected report and enumerated the files they had a reason to open.

**Today.** Counted mechanically rather than by rendering, so this figure is an upper bound and not the
equal of the ten above: the engine behind the public report has twenty-two places where a count is written
into a sentence, and NOT ONE of them has any branch for the singular — against a known-good file measured
the same way in the same breath, which has five such places and a singular branch on all five, so the
count can tell the two apart. One example, quoted: `${total - titleOk} pagini au title tag lipsa` renders
`1 pagini au title tag lipsa`. How many of the twenty-two can actually reach a count of one has not been
measured.

**Fix.** Not decided. This row is OPEN and carries the two readings rather than a decision — see question 2.
Either the check from row 1 covers all client-facing text and these are repaired here, or it covers all
client-facing text and merely REPORTS them for the next contract, or its scope stops at the connected
report and this row is struck.

**Done when.** Cannot be written until question 2 is answered. Whatever the answer, the number of unguarded
places in the public report is stated in this contract before it closes — including if the answer is that
we leave them, because "we looked and chose not to" and "nobody looked" must not be the same record.

**Proving this creates nothing.** The public report is generated from a website address only.

## 5. The report contradicts itself about search terms when there is exactly one

**What this is.** Part of the report says what people actually typed into Google before clicking. Because
Google hides most of these, the report carries a caveat whenever we cannot see them, saying so honestly
rather than pretending the account is clean.

**Problem.** The test for "can we see the search terms" asks whether there is more than one, when it should
ask whether there is more than none. An account with exactly one visible term gets the caveat saying we
have no visibility — printed directly underneath a finding built from that very term.

**Today.** Live in the code on the site, and the two sentences appear on the same screen contradicting each
other whenever an account returns exactly one term.

**Fix.** The test asks whether there is at least one.

**Done when.** With an account returning exactly one search term, the report shows the finding and does NOT
show the caveat; with an account returning none, it shows the caveat. Both arms are checked — the second is
what stops this being repaired by deleting the caveat. Then read on the live site.

**Proving this creates nothing.** Obtaining a live report is question 1.

## 6. Two tests that cannot fail are worse than no tests

**What this is.** Two tests shipped on 21 August to protect work delivered the same day: one guards a
sentence about a single dead product, the other guards the legend that explains our three honesty labels.

**Problem.** Neither can fail. The first wraps everything it checks inside a condition, so if the finding it
is about is missing altogether, the test passes having checked nothing. The second matches words that also
appear elsewhere on the page, so deleting the thing it guards leaves it green.

**Today.** Proved, not suspected: a legend was planted carrying three bold labels and no explanations at
all, and 11 of 11 tests passed.

**Fix.** Both are rewritten so that removing what they guard makes them fail.

**Done when.** For each of the two: the thing it guards is removed, the test is run and FAILS naming it, and
the thing is put back. Written down with what fell, per the house rule that a green test proves nothing.

**Proving this creates nothing.** It runs against the code.

**Why this is in a contract about the report and not filed as a process improvement:** these two tests are
the entire proof that work already called finished was actually done. A finding about an instrument is
filed when the instrument could merely be better, and holds the work open when its defect means a row's
proof was never obtained. This is the second.

## 7. One message, two wordings, and they will drift

**What this is.** The report ends with a legend explaining our three honesty labels — measured, estimate,
projection — because the report puts one of those labels on every figure. The same explanation already
exists, worded differently, on the page a prospect lands on before he ever gets a report.

**Problem.** The same message is written twice in two places. The version on the landing page is the better
one. Two wordings for one message diverge, and then two people who read us in two places are told two
different things about how honest our numbers are.

**Today.** Both live. The report's wording is supported by the specification and is not wrong — it is
merely the weaker of two, and the weaker one is the one a client reads at the moment he decides whether to
believe the numbers above it.

**Fix.** One wording, read from one place by both screens.

**Done when.** On the live site, the sentence explaining the labels is character-for-character the same in
the report and on the landing page, and there is only one place in the code it can be changed.

**Proving this creates nothing.** Both pages are public; the landing page needs no account at all, so half
of this row can be proved on the live site with no session and no question 1.

## 8. New comments in the wrong language, with nothing marking the debt

**What this is.** The house rule is that everything written into a file is in English, whatever language the
conversation was in, and that a file never changes language halfway — if you must add in the file's own
language, you mark it for translation in the same edit.

**Problem.** Comments added on 21 August went in in the wrong language for their file, and nothing was
marked.

**Today.** In the code that is on the branch, unmarked.

**Fix.** Translated, or marked as a named debt in the same edit. Not left as an inconsistency the next
reader has to interpret.

**Done when.** The file is in one language, or carries the marker that says which part is not yet
translated and that this is known.

**Proving this creates nothing.**

## 9. The linter has been failing on the site for longer than this work

**What this is.** The house requires the linter to be run before any front-end deploy, because the
type-checker does not catch what it catches.

**Problem.** The linter exits with a failure, so the rule that says "run the linter before deploying" has
nothing to report except a failure that everyone steps over — which is how a real error will get stepped
over too.

**Today.** Two errors, in TWO different files, not one — a quotation mark that must be escaped on one
screen, and a value reassigned during rendering on another. Both were already there before any of this
work started and neither file was touched by it. **The build does NOT fail on them:** the site builds
cleanly, exit 0, because the build tool in use here no longer runs the linter at all. So this is not
blocking the deploy, and it is not a dependency of any other row — it is a rule that has quietly stopped
being enforceable.

**Fix.** Both errors repaired, so that "the linter passes" goes back to being a statement that can be
checked before a deploy.

**Done when.** The linter exits zero — the exit code read directly, not read off the end of a pipe, which
reports the exit code of whatever the output was piped into.

**Proving this creates nothing.**

# The questions that are still open

These are numbered because they need answers before or at signature. Rows 2, 3, 4 and 5 cannot be fully
closed until question 1 is answered.

## Question 1 — how does a row get proved on the live site?

Every row now ends with its test run on production. For this tool, seeing the report at all needs a
session, and a session needs either a connected Google Ads account or the demonstration mode. The
demonstration mode is switched on by a setting on the server; whether it is switched on on the live site is
not something this contract could measure from here.

1. **Turn the demonstration mode on on the live site (recommended).** Gives: any row provable on the live
   site, at any count, repeatably, by anyone, with no client's account involved — and the report already
   prints a visible banner saying the figures are simulated, so nobody can mistake it for real. Costs: a
   simulated report becomes reachable by a stranger who finds the address.
2. **Prove on the live site by connecting one of our own accounts, read-only.** Gives: real data, no new
   setting. Costs: it only proves the branches that account's numbers happen to hit — and this whole
   contract is about what happens at a count of one, which a real account rarely has. It also creates an
   access grant that has to be revoked afterwards, so it is the only proof here that creates anything.
3. **Narrow what "proved on live" means for this contract.** Live proof = the page renders on production and
   the sentences visible with real data are quoted; the one-and-two matrix is proved before deploy and each
   row says so. Gives: honest and cheap. Costs: the strongest claim we are making is never checked on the
   live site.

Recommendation is 1, because the whole contract is about behaviour at counts a real account will not
produce on demand, and option 2 cannot reach them.

## Question 2 — does the check cover the public report as well?

1. **Yes, and the public report is repaired in this contract (recommended).** Gives: the class is closed
   wherever a client reads us, and the public report is where a stranger reads us FIRST. Costs: up to
   twenty-two more places, roughly doubling the text work, on a screen neither review opened and which
   another specification governs.
2. **Yes, but the public report is only measured and reported here, repaired next.** Gives: the class is
   closed and the size of what remains is written down instead of guessed at. Costs: a page we know is
   wrong stays wrong for another contract.
3. **No — the check stops at the connected report.** Gives: the smallest contract. Costs: the same defect
   stays live and unguarded on the more public of our two reports, and whoever finds it next will find it
   the way it was found today, by accident.

Recommendation is 2 — it closes the class without doubling a contract whose measured material is entirely
about the connected report, and it makes the remainder a number rather than a suspicion.

## Question 3 — does the check belong in this contract, or in a second one?

1. **In this contract, as row 1 (recommended).** Gives: the twelve repairs get proved by something instead
   of by eye, and the check can be proved the day it is written, because ten known-broken sentences make it
   fail and twenty-three known-good ones make it pass — that is a rare and cheap piece of luck. Costs: the
   contract opens with work a client cannot see.
2. **A second contract.** Gives: the twelve sentences reach a client sooner. Costs: nothing requires the
   thirteenth to be right. Today, while writing this, a third file with the same defect was found in a
   place nobody had looked — which is the argument, not a worry about it.

Recommendation is 1.

## Question 4 — is `approved:` empty on purpose here?

A standing authorisation from 17 August is on record in the gate's own configuration, and a contract whose
approval reads STANDING is accepted on it. This contract ships with the field EMPTY, deliberately, because
the instruction that produced it said so and because an agent must never write a signature. Both are
available:

1. **Sign it in your own words (recommended).** Gives: the one reading of a numbered list that catches a
   wrong row before both reviewers start taking their yardstick from it. Costs: one read.
2. **Write STANDING.** Gives: work starts immediately. Costs: nobody reads the list first, and this contract
   carries four open questions and one OPEN row that would then resolve to a recommendation rather than to
   your decision.
